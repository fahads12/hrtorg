Skin Changes

Dryness, thinning, loss of elasticity, and new sensitivity are common hormonal signals — especially in perimenopause and andropause.

Overview

Estrogen is directly involved in collagen production, skin thickness, and sebum regulation. The ~30% decline in collagen in the first five years of menopause explains why dryness, thinning, and new sensitivity show up roughly at the same time as hot flashes. Men experience a slower parallel decline as testosterone and DHEA fall, affecting elasticity and healing speed. Treatment addresses the hormone layer plus targeted topical care.

What's driving skin changes

Estrogen receptors are everywhere in skin — in fibroblasts (collagen-producing cells), keratinocytes, and sebaceous glands. Declining estrogen reduces collagen synthesis, slows wound healing, and shifts sebum balance. Topical retinoids and peptides help, but if the hormonal substrate is low, topicals alone can't restore skin thickness or elasticity.

What to test

A full hormone panel (estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, DHEA-S) alongside thyroid testing identifies the drivers. Low thyroid can present as dry, puffy skin independent of sex hormones. Nutrient testing (vitamin D, zinc, essential fatty acids) completes the picture.

Treatment options

Transdermal estradiol often improves skin thickness, hydration, and elasticity meaningfully within 3-6 months. Local vaginal estrogen addresses GSM. Low-dose testosterone (compounded for women) can further improve sebum balance and skin tone. Topical additions: retinoids, peptides (copper, BPC-157), vitamin C, and physical sunscreen.
