Overview

Erectile dysfunction — difficulty achieving or maintaining an erection sufficient for satisfying sexual activity — affects an estimated 40% of men by age 40 and climbs with age. It's rarely "just psychological" and rarely "just aging." For most men, ED is a measurable mechanical, hormonal, or vascular issue with specific treatments. It's also an early warning sign of broader cardiovascular health that's worth taking seriously beyond the sexual symptom.

Three pathways to ED

Erection is a coordinated event requiring:

  • Vascular inflow — healthy blood flow into the penile corpora via arteries that dilate on cue.
  • Hormonal signal — testosterone to maintain libido and nitric oxide signaling.
  • Neurologic coordination — autonomic nervous system firing in response to arousal.

Problems in any of these pathways can cause ED. Most men have one dominant cause plus contributions from the others. Treating only the obvious one often misses the real driver.

ED that develops gradually over months to years is usually vascular or hormonal. ED that appears abruptly is often psychological or medication-related.

Common causes

Vascular (the biggest category):

  • Atherosclerosis — the same process affecting coronary arteries affects penile arteries, often earlier.
  • Endothelial dysfunction — reduced nitric oxide response from metabolic or inflammatory conditions.
  • Diabetes — both vascular and neurologic damage.
  • Hypertension and its medications.

Hormonal:

  • Low testosterone — reduces libido and nitric oxide production.
  • Elevated prolactin — suppresses testosterone.
  • Thyroid dysfunction (both hypo and hyper).

Neurologic:

  • Diabetic neuropathy
  • Spinal cord injury
  • Prostate surgery (often temporary with recovery)
  • Multiple sclerosis or other neurologic disease

Psychogenic / situational: performance anxiety, relationship stress, depression. Classic sign: morning erections intact but situational ED.

Medication-induced: SSRIs, beta-blockers, finasteride (uncommon), some antihypertensives, opioids.

Workup

A good ED evaluation covers:

  • History — onset, timing, morning erections, psychological context, medications, cardiovascular risk factors.
  • Hormone labs — total and free testosterone (AM draw), SHBG, estradiol, thyroid panel, prolactin if clinically indicated.
  • Metabolic labs — HbA1c, lipids, fasting glucose.
  • Blood pressure — often reveals untreated or undertreated hypertension.
  • Cardiovascular risk assessment — ED can precede symptomatic cardiovascular disease by 3–5 years. A man presenting with new ED and cardiovascular risk factors warrants a cardiology referral.

First-line treatment: PDE5 inhibitors

Sildenafil (Viagra), tadalafil (Cialis), vardenafil (Levitra), and avanafil (Stendra) all work the same way — they inhibit the enzyme PDE5 that breaks down cGMP, the signaling molecule that keeps penile arteries dilated during an erection. They don't cause erections; they allow erections to happen in response to arousal.

Comparing the main options:

  • Sildenafil (Viagra): onset 30–60 min, duration 4–6 hours. Food can delay onset. Classic "take when needed" option.
  • Tadalafil (Cialis): onset 1–2 hours, duration up to 36 hours ("the weekend pill"). Also available as low-dose daily (2.5–5 mg) for flexibility.
  • Generics (sildenafil, tadalafil): same molecule, substantially lower cost. Standard first-line.

Response rate is ~70% for men with vascular ED. Doesn't work? Often means wrong dose, timing, or an underlying issue (low T, undiagnosed metabolic disease) that needs addressing.

Second-line and beyond

Testosterone replacement — if labs show clinically low T, TRT often restores response to PDE5 inhibitors even when they didn't work alone.

Trimix injections — compounded blend of papaverine, phentolamine, and alprostadil injected directly into the penis. Works mechanically regardless of neurologic or vascular status. Very effective but requires injection training and comfort.

PT-141 (bremelanotide) — a melanocortin peptide that acts centrally to increase arousal and desire. Works differently from PDE5 inhibitors; useful for men who need both arousal and erectile support.

Shockwave therapy (LI-ESWT) — low-intensity extracorporeal shockwaves promote angiogenesis in penile tissue. Evidence is strongest for mild-to-moderate vascular ED. Delivered as a series of in-office treatments.

Vacuum erection devices — mechanical option, often used alongside other treatments.

Penile implant — surgical option for men who fail all other treatments. Reserved for severe cases.

Frequently asked questions

Which is better — Viagra or Cialis?

Depends on your preferences. Viagra (sildenafil) is faster-on, shorter-acting, less expensive generically. Cialis (tadalafil) has a longer window and can be dosed daily for ongoing readiness. Most men try both and settle on the one that fits their lifestyle. Many use both — Cialis daily low-dose with Viagra for high-opportunity moments.

Do I need to check my testosterone?

Yes. Low testosterone contributes to ED and reduces response to PDE5 inhibitors. We check total T, free T, SHBG, and estradiol as part of every ED workup. If low, TRT often restores response to PDE5 inhibitors even when they didn't work alone.

Is ED a sign of heart disease?

New-onset ED in a man under 60 is a well-documented early warning sign of cardiovascular disease — often preceding symptomatic coronary disease by 3–5 years. Any man presenting with ED should have cardiovascular risk assessed and treated.

What if PDE5 inhibitors don't work?

Non-response is often fixable. Check testosterone (if low, TRT often restores response). Try the maximum effective dose. Consider timing (empty stomach for sildenafil). If still no response: Trimix, PT-141, shockwave, or implant become the next steps. Most men with ED can achieve satisfactory function with the right workup and sequence.
