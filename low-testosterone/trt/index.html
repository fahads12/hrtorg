Overview

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) restores physiological testosterone levels in men whose own production has fallen below a clinically meaningful threshold. Done well, it addresses the symptoms of low testosterone — reduced energy, libido, muscle mass, cognitive clarity, and mood — while carefully monitoring the downstream effects on red blood cell count, estrogen conversion, fertility, and cardiovascular markers. Done poorly, TRT is one of the most over-prescribed and under-monitored interventions in men's health. The difference is in the protocol.

How TRT works

Testosterone is produced in the testes under signaling from the pituitary gland (LH and FSH). Starting around age 30, testosterone production declines approximately 1% per year in most men. By 60, roughly 30% of men meet clinical criteria for low T.

TRT delivers exogenous testosterone to restore serum levels into the mid-to-upper half of the normal reference range (typically 500–900 ng/dL for total testosterone, with free T in the upper quartile). Routes include:

  • Intramuscular or subcutaneous injection (cypionate, enanthate) — most common, dosed weekly or twice weekly for stable levels.
  • Topical cream or gel — daily application; smoother pharmacokinetics; transferable to partners, which is a safety consideration.
  • Subcutaneous pellets — implanted every 3–6 months; set-it-and-forget-it but requires an in-office procedure.
  • Oral testosterone undecanoate — newer, FDA-approved in 2019; convenient but costlier.

Each has tradeoffs in cost, convenience, and pharmacokinetic profile. Your provider helps match the format to your lifestyle, goals, and clinical picture.

Who TRT is for

Clinical TRT candidacy requires both labs AND symptoms. Labs alone (low number, no symptoms) or symptoms alone (symptoms, labs in range) are typically not enough to justify treatment.

Lab thresholds:

  • Total testosterone below ~300 ng/dL on two separate AM draws.
  • Low or inappropriately normal LH suggests primary vs. secondary hypogonadism.
  • Free testosterone and SHBG inform how much testosterone is actually bioavailable.

Symptomatic criteria:

  • Reduced libido or erectile changes
  • Fatigue not explained by sleep or lifestyle
  • Lost muscle mass or increased body fat
  • Depressed mood, irritability, cognitive flattening
  • Loss of morning erections

Before starting TRT, we evaluate for reversible causes: medications (opioids, SSRIs, glucocorticoids), sleep apnea, obesity, chronic stress, iron deficiency. If any of those are modifiable, we often treat them first.

What to expect on TRT

Response unfolds over months:

  • Weeks 1–4: energy, mood, and libido often improve first.
  • Months 2–3: cognitive clarity and sleep quality typically improve.
  • Months 3–6: body composition starts to shift — more lean mass, less fat — especially with strength training.
  • Months 6–12: full effect; this is where we re-evaluate protocol and labs.

Not every symptom responds equally. Libido and energy are the most reliable responders. Mood and cognition tend to improve more gradually. Fat loss and muscle gain require consistent training and nutrition — TRT amplifies the work; it doesn't replace it.

Side effects and monitoring

Common and manageable:

  • Elevated hematocrit (red blood cell concentration) — the most common lab change. Managed with dose adjustment or periodic blood donation.
  • Elevated estradiol from aromatization of testosterone. May require an aromatase inhibitor (anastrozole) if symptoms develop.
  • Acne or oily skin — common early, usually settles.
  • Mild sleep disturbance — occasional, usually transient.
  • Testicular atrophy — exogenous testosterone suppresses LH/FSH, which shrinks the testes and halts sperm production.

Less common: hair loss acceleration in men with androgenic alopecia predisposition (DHT conversion); gynecomastia if estradiol rises significantly; mood changes.

Monitoring schedule (standard):

  • Baseline: total T, free T, SHBG, estradiol, CBC, PSA, lipids, metabolic panel.
  • 8–12 weeks after starting: repeat T, free T, estradiol, hematocrit. Adjust dose.
  • 6 months: full panel + PSA. Adjust.
  • Annually: full panel + PSA.

Modern evidence does not support the historical concern that TRT causes prostate cancer. However, TRT can accelerate growth of an existing undiagnosed prostate cancer, so we screen PSA at baseline and annually.

Dosing and formats

Typical starting doses by format:

  • Testosterone cypionate (IM or SubQ): 100–200 mg once weekly, or split into two 50–100 mg doses twice weekly for smoother levels.
  • Testosterone enanthate: similar to cypionate; slightly shorter half-life.
  • Testosterone cream: 50–100 mg daily applied to shoulders or inner thighs.
  • Testosterone pellets: 600–1200 mg implanted every 3–6 months.

We prefer twice-weekly injections over once-weekly for most patients because of smoother levels and lower estradiol conversion peaks. Pellets are convenient but less flexible for dose adjustment.

Frequently asked questions

Will TRT make me infertile?

Yes — exogenous testosterone suppresses LH and FSH, which shuts down natural testicular function including sperm production. If fertility is a priority, we discuss sperm banking before starting, OR we use fertility-preserving protocols (hCG, enclomiphene, Clomid) that raise your own testosterone while maintaining sperm production.

Is TRT a lifelong commitment?

Not necessarily — but exogenous testosterone suppresses natural production, so stopping requires a careful plan. Some men cycle off with HCG and clomiphene; others stay on indefinitely. We revisit annually based on your goals.

What about the cardiovascular safety concerns?

Historical concerns about TRT and cardiovascular risk have been largely refuted by the 2023 TRAVERSE trial, which showed TRT in hypogonadal men did not increase major adverse cardiovascular events compared with placebo. We still monitor lipids, blood pressure, and hematocrit carefully.

Is TRT the same as steroids?

TRT restores testosterone to physiological levels — typically 500–900 ng/dL. Anabolic steroid use for performance enhancement pushes levels to 1500+ ng/dL, often with multiple compounds stacked. The difference is clinical oversight, dosing, and goal. TRT is a prescribed medical treatment; "steroids" in the colloquial sense is not.
