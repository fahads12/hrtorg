Finasteride — the gold standard for pattern baldness.

How 5-alpha reductase inhibition works, who benefits, side-effect realities, and when topical makes sense.

Overview

Finasteride is a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor that blocks the conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT) — the androgen primarily responsible for androgenic (male pattern) hair loss. FDA-approved at 1 mg daily for hair loss in 1997 (as Propecia), it remains the most effective single oral treatment for pattern baldness in men. Topical formulations now offer similar efficacy with lower systemic exposure.

How finasteride works

In men with androgenic alopecia, scalp hair follicles are genetically sensitive to DHT. Over time, affected follicles shrink and produce progressively thinner, shorter hairs until only vellus, or peach-fuzz, hair remains. The hairline recedes and the crown thins.

5-alpha reductase is the enzyme that converts testosterone into DHT. Finasteride blocks the type II isoform of this enzyme, reducing circulating DHT by roughly 70%. In the scalp, local DHT drops substantially, which stops follicle miniaturization and often reverses it in follicles not fully miniaturized yet.

Finasteride does not affect testosterone itself. Serum testosterone remains normal or slightly elevated on finasteride; libido and sexual function are intact for most men.

Who finasteride is for

Finasteride is indicated for adult men with:

  • Androgenic alopecia (male pattern hair loss) at any stage
  • Earlier stages respond better than advanced stages — follicles that have fully miniaturized rarely recover.
  • Vertex (crown) thinning responds somewhat better than frontal recession in most studies, but both can improve.

Finasteride is not approved for women. Dutasteride and spironolactone are sometimes used in women, but their hair-loss evaluation often involves different tests, including ferritin, thyroid, an androgen panel, and iron stores.

Not for men with:

  • Prior allergic reaction to finasteride
  • Active prostate cancer concern (finasteride lowers PSA by ~50%, which complicates screening)
  • Pregnancy exposure concerns (women who are or may become pregnant should not handle broken tablets)

What to expect — timeline and results

Hair cycle biology dictates the timeline:

  • Months 1–3: often an initial "shed" as miniaturizing hairs are pushed out before thicker ones grow in. Not failure — the opposite.
  • Months 3–6: stabilization. Hair loss slows noticeably.
  • Months 6–12: regrowth of previously miniaturized hairs becomes visible. Density typically improves 10–30% from baseline.
  • Year 1+: continued stabilization; most improvement happens in the first year.

Trial data at one year: ~65% of men on finasteride show visible improvement in hair density; ~90% show stabilization (no further loss). Only ~10% see no benefit.

Finasteride works best with topical or oral minoxidil. The two treatments act in complementary ways, and together they typically produce a better response than either alone.

If you stop finasteride, the follicles revert to their DHT-sensitive state. Any hair gained usually sheds back to pre-treatment baseline within 6–12 months.

Side effects — what the data shows

Most men tolerate finasteride well. The reported rates of sexual side effects in clinical trials (~1–3%) have been debated in post-market reporting.

Possible side effects:

  • Reduced libido (1–3% in trials; sometimes higher in post-market reports)
  • Erectile changes (1–2%)
  • Decreased ejaculate volume (uncommon)
  • Gynecomastia or breast tenderness (rare)
  • Mood changes (debated; some reports of depression, though causal link remains unclear)

Post-finasteride syndrome (PFS): a controversial reported syndrome of persistent sexual, cognitive, and mood symptoms after stopping finasteride. Prevalence is contested — most urology/dermatology societies consider it rare; patient-reported literature suggests it may be under-recognized. We discuss this openly with every patient before starting.

Men concerned about systemic side effects often start with topical finasteride (compounded 0.1–0.25%) which produces similar scalp DHT reduction with substantially lower systemic exposure.

Dosing and alternatives

Standard dosing:

  • Oral finasteride: 1 mg daily (hair loss indication). The 5 mg dose used for benign prostatic hyperplasia is excessive for hair loss and increases side-effect risk without additional hair benefit.
  • Topical finasteride (compounded): 0.1–0.25% solution applied daily to the scalp, often combined with minoxidil in a single compounded formula.

Alternatives and additions:

  • Minoxidil (topical or oral low-dose) — different mechanism (vasodilation + follicle cycling), highly complementary with finasteride.
  • Dutasteride — more potent 5-alpha reductase inhibitor (blocks both type I and II). Off-label for hair loss but often more effective than finasteride for men who don't fully respond.
  • Microneedling, PRP, LLLT (red light therapy) — adjunctive non-pharmacologic options.
  • Hair transplant — permanent restoration for areas of miniaturization; typically combined with finasteride to prevent further loss elsewhere.

Frequently asked questions

Will finasteride cause erectile dysfunction?

Most men on finasteride don't experience sexual side effects. Clinical trial rates are low (1–3%), though post-market reports are higher. For men concerned about systemic exposure, topical finasteride offers nearly equivalent hair benefit with substantially lower systemic DHT reduction.

Is topical finasteride as effective as oral?

In head-to-head studies, topical finasteride produces similar scalp DHT reduction and similar hair outcomes to oral finasteride, with meaningfully lower serum DHT reduction and lower reported sexual side-effect rates. Many men now prefer topical as a first-line option.

How long do I need to take finasteride?

Indefinitely, if you want to preserve the benefit. Stopping finasteride typically results in resumption of follicle miniaturization and progressive loss over 6–12 months. The decision to continue is reviewed annually.

Does finasteride interact with TRT?

Yes — in a useful way. TRT raises testosterone, which raises DHT. Men prone to androgenic alopecia often see hair loss accelerate on TRT. Co-prescribing finasteride (or topical finasteride) protects the hair while allowing TRT benefits elsewhere.
