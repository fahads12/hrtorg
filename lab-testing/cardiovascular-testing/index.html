Overview

Standard lipid panels (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL, triglycerides) miss roughly half of people who go on to have a heart attack. The reason: LDL-C counts cholesterol inside LDL particles, not the particles themselves. Two people with identical LDL-C can have very different particle counts — and particle count (ApoB) is what actually drives atherosclerosis.

We add ApoB (direct count of atherogenic particles), Lp(a) (an inherited particle that doubles cardiovascular risk when elevated), hs-CRP (vascular inflammation), and homocysteine (methylation / vascular endothelium).

This panel is particularly important before starting hormone therapy (estrogen and testosterone both affect cardiovascular biology), for anyone with family history of early coronary disease, and for anyone over 40.

FAQ

Common questions.

My LDL-C is normal but my ApoB is high — which do I trust?
ApoB. Each atherogenic particle (LDL, VLDL, Lp(a), IDL) carries exactly one ApoB. A patient can have "normal" LDL-C but a large number of small-dense particles — same cholesterol, more plaque. ApoB counts the particles directly.
Should I know my Lp(a)?
Yes — once in your life. It's genetically determined and roughly stable from age 2 onward. If it's above 125 nmol/L, your cardiovascular risk is doubled regardless of other markers. Knowing it changes risk calculations and shapes decisions on PCSK9 inhibitors and aspirin.
What about a CAC scan?
Coronary artery calcium scoring is the single most useful screen for established atherosclerosis in adults 40+. A score of zero at 50 is strongly reassuring; a score above 100 reclassifies you to high-risk regardless of lipids. We often recommend it alongside lipids.
