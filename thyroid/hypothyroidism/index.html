Hypothyroidism — more than TSH.

Why TSH-only screening misses treatable cases, and how proper testing + treatment look.

Overview

Hypothyroidism — an underactive thyroid producing insufficient thyroid hormone — affects an estimated 5–10% of U.S. adults, with higher rates in women and older adults. It's one of the most under-diagnosed and under-treated conditions in primary care, largely because standard screening uses only TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) and misses the subclinical and conversion-related cases where symptoms are real but TSH is "normal."

What hypothyroidism is

The thyroid gland sits at the front of the neck and produces two main hormones:

  • T4 (thyroxine) — the storage form, ~80% of thyroid output.
  • T3 (triiodothyronine) — the active form, which binds thyroid receptors in essentially every cell. Most T3 is converted from T4 in peripheral tissues.

The hypothalamus and pituitary regulate thyroid output via TSH. When thyroid hormone is low, TSH rises to push the thyroid harder. When thyroid hormone is high, TSH falls.

In hypothyroidism, every metabolic process slows: heart rate, resting metabolic rate, cognition, gut motility, hair cycling, body temperature. The symptoms span many systems, which is why diagnosis is often delayed.

Causes

  • Hashimoto's thyroiditis — autoimmune destruction of thyroid tissue. The most common cause in the U.S. Diagnosed by elevated TPO antibodies.
  • Iodine deficiency — a global cause but rare in the U.S. because of iodized salt.
  • Post-partum thyroiditis — transient dysfunction after pregnancy, sometimes permanent.
  • Medications — amiodarone, lithium, interferon, some immunotherapies.
  • Thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine — iatrogenic hypothyroidism.
  • Central hypothyroidism — rare pituitary cause with low TSH + low T4.

Subclinical hypothyroidism (high-normal TSH, normal T4) is a very common finding. Many of these patients are symptomatic and respond to treatment, even though guidelines are ambivalent about treating them.

Symptoms

Common symptoms can occur in any combination:

  • Fatigue, especially persistent despite adequate sleep
  • Weight gain or difficulty losing weight
  • Cold intolerance
  • Dry skin, brittle hair, hair thinning
  • Constipation
  • Depression or flat affect
  • Brain fog, slow thinking, forgetfulness
  • Menstrual irregularity
  • Elevated cholesterol
  • Puffiness around the eyes, swollen hands/feet

Many of these symptoms are nonspecific and get attributed to aging, stress, or lifestyle. Thyroid testing in anyone with multiple of these symptoms is high-yield.

The full thyroid panel

TSH alone does not give a complete picture. A full evaluation includes:

  • TSH — reference range typically 0.4–4.5 µIU/mL. Optimal range is often considered 0.5–2.5.
  • Free T4 — the storage form in its unbound, biologically available fraction.
  • Free T3 — the active hormone. Often low in patients with conversion issues even when TSH and free T4 are "normal."
  • Reverse T3 (rT3) — a biologically inactive isomer of T3 that competes with free T3. Often elevated in stress, illness, or caloric restriction.
  • TPO antibodies (TPOab) — elevated in Hashimoto's. Can be positive for years before TSH rises.
  • Thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAb) — a second autoimmune marker.

Together these catch subclinical hypothyroidism, Hashimoto's before TSH rises, and conversion problems where someone needs T3 rather than just more T4.

Treatment

Levothyroxine (LT4): synthetic T4, the standard of care. Dosed by weight (~1.6 mcg/kg/day typical adult dose) and titrated to TSH response. Taken on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before food or coffee.

Combined LT4 + LT3 (Cytomel): for patients who feel undertreated on LT4 alone despite normalized TSH. Suggests conversion issues. Adding small-dose LT3 (5–10 mcg daily) often restores clinical response.

Natural desiccated thyroid (NDT): porcine thyroid containing both T4 and T3 in a fixed ratio (~4:1). Brands include Armour Thyroid, NP Thyroid, and compounded options. Some patients feel better on NDT than LT4; evidence is mixed but patient-reported outcomes are often favorable.

Compounded T3/T4: flexible-dose compounded formulations allowing custom T3:T4 ratios. Useful when standard products don't work well.

Monitoring: TSH + free T3 + free T4 at 6–8 weeks after any dose change, then every 6–12 months once stable.

Frequently asked questions

My TSH is normal but I have symptoms — what gives?

TSH alone misses several patterns: subclinical hypothyroidism (high-normal TSH with low free T3), conversion issues (normal TSH + T4 but low free T3), and early Hashimoto's (positive TPO antibodies before TSH abnormality). Running the full panel often finds a treatable cause when TSH alone looks fine.

Why do I need T3 if I'm already on levothyroxine?

T4 has to be converted to T3 (the active hormone) in your tissues. Some people are poor converters — their TSH normalizes on LT4 but free T3 stays low and symptoms persist. Adding a small LT3 dose or switching to NDT often fixes this.

Armour or NP Thyroid vs. Synthroid?

Head-to-head studies are mixed. Standard-of-care guidelines still favor LT4 monotherapy. But patient-reported outcomes often favor NDT or combination therapy. Both are reasonable; the right answer is what makes you feel like yourself with labs in a safe range.

Is Hashimoto's curable?

The autoimmune process isn't curable, but it's manageable. Replacement thyroid hormone treats the deficiency. Immune-modulating approaches (diet, gut health, selenium, vitamin D) can reduce TPO antibody levels in some patients, though whether that changes long-term thyroid trajectory remains an active research question.
