Estrogen in menopause.

Estradiol vs. estriol, transdermal vs. oral, systemic vs. vaginal — what to choose and why.

Overview

Estrogen is the central hormone that declines in menopause and the one most responsible for both the symptoms and the long-term consequences of the transition. Understanding what estrogen therapy actually is — which molecule, delivered how, at what dose, and in combination with what — is the foundation of any good HRT plan.

The different estrogens

Three natural estrogens matter clinically:

  • Estradiol (E2) — the most biologically active estrogen; the primary estrogen made by the ovaries in reproductive-age women. Nearly all modern HRT uses estradiol.
  • Estrone (E1) — a weaker estrogen; the dominant form in postmenopausal women (made in adipose tissue from androgens). Not typically prescribed.
  • Estriol (E3) — a very weak estrogen; dominant in pregnancy. Sometimes used in compounded formulations (BiEst or TriEst) for a gentler effect, particularly vaginally.

"Conjugated equine estrogens" (CEE, brand name Premarin) are a mix of estrogen compounds derived from pregnant mare urine. Historically dominant; now less commonly prescribed in favor of bioidentical estradiol.

Delivery routes

Transdermal (patch, gel, cream) — generally preferred. Bypasses hepatic first-pass metabolism, which means lower clotting risk compared with oral. Steady serum levels. Patches change twice weekly; gels and creams applied daily.

Oral tablets — simple, inexpensive. Passes through the liver first, which raises SHBG, triglycerides, and clotting proteins. Generally reserved for women who prefer pills and have no cardiovascular/clotting risk factors.

Vaginal estrogen (cream, tablet, ring) — low-dose local therapy for genitourinary symptoms. Minimal systemic absorption. Can be used safely by most women, including many breast cancer survivors with specialist review. Doesn't require pairing with progesterone because systemic exposure is so low.

Injectable estradiol — rarely used in modern practice; mostly historical.

Pellets — implanted every 3–4 months. Convenient but less flexible for dose adjustment and tend to produce higher serum levels than other routes.

Who estrogen therapy is for

Systemic estrogen is indicated for:

  • Moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats)
  • Prevention of postmenopausal osteoporosis
  • Premature or early menopause (before age 45)
  • Oophorectomy in a premenopausal woman

Low-dose vaginal estrogen is indicated for genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) — vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, recurrent UTIs — in any postmenopausal woman, including those for whom systemic HRT is contraindicated.

Any woman with an intact uterus taking systemic estrogen also needs progesterone to protect the endometrium from unopposed estrogen stimulation.

Dosing and titration

Standard starting doses:

  • Estradiol patch: 0.025 mg/day (ultra-low) to 0.05 mg/day (standard), changed twice weekly. Up-titrate as needed to symptom control.
  • Estradiol gel: 0.5–1 g daily to upper arm or thigh.
  • Estradiol tablet: 0.5–1 mg daily (if oral preferred).
  • Vaginal estradiol cream: 1 g intravaginally 3× weekly after a brief loading phase; or vaginal tablet (10 µg) or ring (7.5 µg/day) with similar efficacy.

Response assessed at 4–8 weeks. If vasomotor symptoms are still bothersome, we titrate up. If side effects appear (breast tenderness, breakthrough bleeding), we titrate down or adjust the progesterone component.

Benefits and risks

Benefits (established):

  • Vasomotor symptom relief (75–90% response)
  • Genitourinary improvement
  • Bone protection
  • When initiated within 10 years of menopause: likely cardiovascular benefit
  • Likely cognitive benefit when initiated early

Risks:

  • Oral estrogen increases VTE and stroke risk modestly; transdermal does not.
  • Breast cancer risk slightly increased with combined estrogen+progestogen after ~5+ years; estrogen alone (for women without a uterus) shows no increase and may slightly reduce risk.
  • Breakthrough bleeding is common in the first 6 months, especially with continuous combined regimens.
  • Breast tenderness and bloating, usually transient.

Monitoring

We check:

  • Symptoms at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and annually.
  • Blood pressure and basic metabolic panel at baseline and annually.
  • Mammography per guidelines.
  • DEXA scan baseline (if risk factors) and every 2 years when on long-term HRT.
  • Serum estradiol level is sometimes checked at 3 months to confirm absorption, but symptom response is the primary endpoint.

Any abnormal vaginal bleeding after the first 6 months on HRT needs evaluation.

Frequently asked questions

Patch, gel, or pill — which is best?

For most women, the transdermal patch is the first choice because of its clean pharmacokinetics and lower clotting risk compared with oral. Gel is a reasonable alternative if patches don't stick well or cause skin irritation. Oral tablets are fine if you have no cardiovascular risk factors and prefer pills.

Do I need progesterone if I'm on estrogen?

Yes, if you have a uterus. Unopposed estrogen stimulates the endometrial lining and substantially raises risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer. If you've had a hysterectomy, estrogen alone is appropriate.

Is vaginal estrogen safe long-term?

Yes. Vaginal estrogen at standard low doses produces minimal systemic absorption and is considered safe for long-term use in most women, including many breast cancer survivors with specialist coordination. It's one of the most effective and under-prescribed treatments in menopausal care.

What about BiEst (estradiol + estriol)?

BiEst is a compounded blend of estradiol and estriol in a chosen ratio (commonly 20:80 or 50:50). Some clinicians favor it for a gentler profile; others prefer standardized FDA-approved estradiol. Both can be reasonable; your provider helps match the format to your preferences and clinical picture.
