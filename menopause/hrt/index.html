HRT: the modern evidence.

Who benefits, how it's dosed, and why 2000s-era fears don't match current data.

Overview

Hormone replacement therapy for menopause is one of the most-studied, most-misunderstood interventions in women's health. The 2002 Women's Health Initiative generated a wave of fear that led to a generation of women avoiding HRT; subsequent re-analysis and new trials have substantially clarified the picture. Current evidence supports HRT as the most effective treatment for menopausal vasomotor symptoms, with a favorable benefit-risk profile for most women when started within 10 years of menopause and tailored to the individual.

What HRT actually is

Modern HRT typically combines two hormones:

  • Estradiol (the primary estrogen) — delivered transdermally (patch, gel, cream) or orally. Transdermal is preferred for most women because it bypasses hepatic first-pass metabolism, reducing clotting-related risk.
  • Progesterone (specifically micronized progesterone, prescribed as Prometrium or compounded) — necessary for any woman with a uterus to protect the endometrium from unopposed estrogen stimulation.

Testosterone and DHEA are sometimes added at low doses when indicated by symptoms and labs — primarily for libido, energy, or body composition.

"Bioidentical" means the hormone molecule is identical to what your body produces. FDA-approved bioidenticals exist (estradiol patch, micronized progesterone capsules). Compounded bioidenticals are custom-formulated by compounding pharmacies. Both can be clinically appropriate.

Who HRT is for

Primary indication: moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats, flushing) in women within 10 years of menopause onset or under age 60. These symptoms respond to HRT more reliably than to any other intervention.

Secondary indications:

  • Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (vaginal dryness, painful intercourse, recurrent UTIs) — often responds to local vaginal estrogen alone.
  • Prevention of postmenopausal bone loss.
  • Early menopause (natural or surgical before age 45) — HRT is generally recommended until the average age of natural menopause (~51).
  • Mood, sleep, and cognitive symptoms when they track with the menopausal transition.

Contraindications: active or history of estrogen-dependent cancer (breast, endometrial), active thromboembolic disease, active liver disease, undiagnosed abnormal vaginal bleeding, known hypersensitivity.

Relative cautions: history of migraine with aura, significant cardiovascular disease, strong family history of breast cancer. These require individualized discussion.

Benefits — what the evidence shows

Vasomotor symptom relief: 75–90% of women experience substantial reduction in hot flashes and night sweats within 4–8 weeks. This is the strongest and most consistent benefit.

Genitourinary symptoms: dryness, pain with intercourse, and recurrent UTIs typically improve within 1–3 months. Local vaginal estrogen alone is highly effective here with minimal systemic absorption.

Bone health: estrogen is one of the strongest known interventions to prevent postmenopausal bone loss and reduce fracture risk.

Cardiovascular: when HRT is initiated within 10 years of menopause ("the window of opportunity"), it appears to reduce coronary heart disease risk. Starting HRT 20+ years postmenopause does not show the same benefit.

Mood and cognition: many women report improved mood stability, sleep, and cognitive clarity, though these effects are more variable and harder to study rigorously.

Risks — understanding them accurately

Breast cancer risk: combined estrogen + progestin modestly increases breast cancer risk after approximately 5+ years of use (absolute risk of ~1 extra case per 1,000 women per year). Estrogen alone (for women without a uterus) shows no increased risk and may slightly reduce it in some analyses. The type of progestogen matters — micronized progesterone appears less associated with breast cancer than synthetic progestins used in older trials.

Clotting (VTE): oral estrogen modestly increases risk. Transdermal estrogen appears to carry little or no VTE risk because it bypasses hepatic coagulation effects.

Stroke and cardiovascular: risk depends heavily on age at initiation. Starting within 10 years of menopause appears neutral or protective; starting after 60–65 may slightly increase risk.

Endometrial cancer: estrogen alone in women with a uterus substantially increases risk. Always paired with progesterone to prevent this.

Modern HRT is lower-risk than the 2002 WHI protocol because of transdermal estrogen, bioidentical progesterone, and lower effective doses.

Starting HRT — practical protocol

A typical starting regimen for a woman in her early 50s with vasomotor symptoms:

  • Estradiol patch 0.025–0.05 mg twice weekly, titrated to symptom control.
  • Oral micronized progesterone 100–200 mg at bedtime (also supports sleep).
  • Response usually clear within 4–8 weeks. Full dose optimization typically settles by 3 months.

We re-evaluate at 3 months (symptoms, side effects, bleeding pattern) and 12 months (labs if indicated, breast health screening, continuation decision). Many women stay on HRT for 5–10 years or longer; the decision is individualized based on benefit and preference.

Frequently asked questions

Isn't HRT dangerous? What about the WHI?

The original 2002 Women's Health Initiative used a specific regimen (oral conjugated equine estrogens + medroxyprogesterone acetate) in a mostly older population (mean age ~63). Subsequent re-analyses showed the risk profile looks very different for women starting within 10 years of menopause, and modern transdermal estradiol + bioidentical progesterone has a more favorable profile than the WHI regimen.

How long should I stay on HRT?

There's no fixed stopping date. Many women use HRT for 5–10 years for symptom control. Some continue indefinitely for ongoing bone, cardiovascular, and quality-of-life benefits. The decision gets individualized at each annual review.

Can I use HRT if I had breast cancer?

Standard HRT is contraindicated after breast cancer. We offer non-hormonal protocols (SSRIs, gabapentin, lifestyle) and, with specialist review, sometimes local vaginal estrogen for genitourinary symptoms. Always discussed in coordination with your oncology team.

What's the difference between bioidentical and conventional HRT?

"Bioidentical" means the hormone molecule is structurally identical to what your body makes. FDA-approved bioidentical estradiol (patch, gel) and micronized progesterone (Prometrium) are both bioidentical. Compounded bioidenticals are custom-formulated and allow dose flexibility but lack the standardization of FDA-approved products. Both can be appropriate; your provider helps you choose.
