Progesterone, often under-prescribed.

Beyond endometrial protection — the sleep, mood, and anxiety benefits that make progesterone useful on its own.

Overview

Progesterone is the under-appreciated half of hormone replacement therapy. It's required alongside estrogen for any woman with a uterus (to protect the endometrium), but its independent benefits for sleep, anxiety, mood, and bone health are often overlooked. The specific form of progesterone matters — bioidentical micronized progesterone (Prometrium or compounded) has a notably different clinical profile from older synthetic progestins like medroxyprogesterone acetate.

Why progesterone matters

In cycling women, progesterone peaks in the luteal phase (post-ovulation) and is responsible for cycle regulation, pregnancy maintenance, and the calming "GABAergic" effect on the nervous system. In perimenopause, progesterone becomes erratic and often insufficient even before estrogen levels start to fall meaningfully. The result: new sleep problems, anxiety, and irritability — often years before "classic" menopausal symptoms begin.

Progesterone is also an essential partner to estrogen in HRT. Unopposed estrogen in a woman with a uterus causes endometrial hyperplasia and raises cancer risk; progesterone shed cycles the lining or keeps it thin.

Bioidentical micronized progesterone vs. synthetic progestins

"Progesterone" and "progestin" aren't interchangeable:

  • Progesterone — the human hormone. "Micronized progesterone" is the bioidentical form, FDA-approved as Prometrium (oral capsules) and available compounded as cream, oral, vaginal, or injection. The structure is identical to what your ovaries made in your reproductive years.
  • Progestins — synthetic analogs. Medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA, the drug used in the WHI trial) and norethindrone are examples. They activate the progesterone receptor but have additional off-target effects.

Modern practice generally prefers bioidentical micronized progesterone over synthetic progestins because of:

  • Better side-effect profile (less bloating, mood effects, breast tenderness)
  • Favorable sleep effects (particularly with oral dosing at bedtime)
  • Lower cardiovascular risk
  • Likely lower breast cancer signal — the WHI MPA cohort showed increased risk; subsequent large observational studies with micronized progesterone show less or no increase.

Delivery routes and their uses

Oral micronized progesterone (Prometrium capsules or compounded): most common route. Dosed at bedtime because of its sedating effect — a feature, not a bug, for perimenopausal women with insomnia. First-pass metabolism produces allopregnanolone, which is responsible for the sleep benefit.

Vaginal progesterone (cream, suppository, or gel): higher endometrial exposure, less systemic effect. Favored for women who want endometrial protection without the sedating effect.

Topical progesterone cream: popular but controversial — systemic absorption is highly variable, and endometrial protection may be inadequate at common OTC doses. We use compounded transdermal progesterone selectively and generally not as the sole endometrial protector for women on systemic estrogen.

Injectable: rarely used in modern HRT practice.

Dosing patterns

Cyclic (perimenopausal women still having periods):

  • 100–200 mg oral micronized progesterone at bedtime, days 14–28 of each cycle.
  • Mimics the natural luteal phase; supports cycle regularity and endometrial shedding.

Continuous combined (postmenopausal women on estrogen):

  • 100 mg oral micronized progesterone at bedtime daily, OR
  • 200 mg oral micronized progesterone at bedtime 12 days per month (continuous sequential).
  • Both protect the endometrium; continuous daily avoids monthly withdrawal bleeding, which many postmenopausal women prefer.

Progesterone alone (perimenopausal, estrogen-sufficient women with sleep/anxiety issues):

  • 100 mg oral at bedtime, nightly or cycled.
  • Often the first HRT intervention we recommend for perimenopausal women whose primary symptoms are sleep and anxiety rather than hot flashes.

Benefits beyond endometrial protection

Sleep: oral micronized progesterone at bedtime shortens sleep latency, deepens sleep architecture, and reduces 3 AM awakenings — effects well-documented in sleep lab studies.

Anxiety: progesterone's metabolite allopregnanolone acts on GABA receptors with calming effects. Women with new-onset perimenopausal anxiety often respond notably well.

Mood: less consistent but reports frequent improvement in irritability and emotional lability during perimenopause.

Bone: progesterone supports osteoblast activity (bone-building), complementing estrogen's anti-resorptive effect.

Cardiovascular: micronized progesterone appears neutral or possibly beneficial for lipids and blood pressure, in contrast to some synthetic progestins.

Side effects and safety

Common: drowsiness (dose at bedtime), mild dizziness, breast tenderness, bloating.

Less common: headache, mood changes (some women are "paradoxical responders" and feel worse, not better).

Rare: allergic reactions.

If a woman does badly on oral micronized progesterone (uncommon but real), we can try vaginal delivery or alternative progestogens with provider guidance.

Progesterone is pregnancy category B — safe and often prescribed in pregnancy. Not associated with breast cancer in the way some synthetic progestins have been.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take progesterone alone, without estrogen?

Yes. In perimenopausal women with sleep and anxiety issues but normal estrogen, progesterone alone is often the right first step. In postmenopausal women on systemic estrogen, progesterone is required alongside the estrogen for endometrial protection.

Is OTC progesterone cream enough for HRT?

OTC progesterone creams often lack sufficient endometrial exposure to protect a woman on systemic estrogen. Prescription-strength oral or vaginal progesterone is generally preferred. For symptom relief alone (without concurrent estrogen), compounded prescription creams may work but are less predictable than oral dosing.

Will progesterone make me gain weight?

Not typically. Some women notice mild bloating or water retention in the first few months, usually resolving on its own. Significant weight gain on progesterone alone is uncommon and worth evaluating for other causes.

Does progesterone cause breast cancer?

Micronized bioidentical progesterone has not been linked to breast cancer in large observational studies the way synthetic progestins (particularly MPA) have been. Most modern reviews consider micronized progesterone the lower-risk option when progestogen is needed.
