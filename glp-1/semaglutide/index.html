Semaglutide, explained.

The GLP-1 agonist behind Ozempic and Wegovy — mechanism, outcomes, and what to expect.

Overview

Semaglutide is a once-weekly injectable GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonist first FDA-approved for type 2 diabetes in 2017 (Ozempic) and later for chronic weight management in 2021 (Wegovy). It's a synthetic analog of a natural gut hormone your body produces after eating. By mimicking that signal more durably than the native hormone, semaglutide reduces appetite, slows gastric emptying, and improves blood glucose control — making it one of the most studied and effective pharmacologic tools for weight loss in a generation.

How semaglutide works

GLP-1 is a hormone released by L-cells in the small intestine after you eat. Its job is to tell the pancreas to release insulin, tell the stomach to empty more slowly, and tell the brain you're satisfied. The natural hormone is short-lived — broken down within minutes by an enzyme called DPP-4.

Semaglutide is engineered to resist that breakdown. A single injection remains active for about a week, keeping GLP-1 signaling steady. Four mechanisms drive the weight-loss effect:

  • Appetite suppression — semaglutide binds GLP-1 receptors in the hypothalamus, reducing hunger signals and food noise.
  • Delayed gastric emptying — food sits in the stomach longer, extending the feeling of fullness after a meal.
  • Insulin sensitization — pancreatic beta cells release more insulin in response to glucose, improving metabolic control.
  • Reduced hepatic glucose output — the liver releases less sugar between meals, which flattens blood-sugar swings.

The combined effect is lower hunger, smaller portions by choice, and better insulin response — all of which translate to a sustained calorie deficit without the hunger that typically ends diets.

Who semaglutide is for

FDA labeling for weight management (Wegovy) requires one of the following:

  • BMI ≥ 30, or
  • BMI ≥ 27 with at least one weight-related condition (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol, obstructive sleep apnea, cardiovascular disease).

Clinically, our providers also prescribe for patients who've tried structured lifestyle approaches without durable success, patients with insulin resistance or prediabetes, and patients with PCOS where metabolic improvement is a treatment goal.

Semaglutide is not appropriate if you have:

  • Personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN2) — these are absolute contraindications based on preclinical thyroid tumor signals.
  • History of pancreatitis — requires careful provider review.
  • Active pregnancy, breastfeeding, or active trying to conceive.
  • Severe gastroparesis or GI motility disorder.
  • Type 1 diabetes (our telehealth doesn't cover T1D protocols).

What to expect — results and timeline

Semaglutide dosing titrates slowly to minimize side effects. Standard schedule starts at 0.25 mg once weekly, increasing every four weeks (0.5 → 1.0 → 1.7 → 2.4 mg). Most patients reach a therapeutic dose by month 4.

What outcomes look like in trials: the STEP program (Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with obesity) showed mean weight loss of 14.9% of body weight at 68 weeks on 2.4 mg weekly, compared with ~2.4% in the placebo group. Roughly a third of patients lost ≥20% of body weight. Diabetic patients (SUSTAIN trials) showed meaningful HbA1c reductions alongside weight loss.

Realistic monthly expectations:

  • Month 1 (0.25 mg): modest appetite reduction, 2–5 lbs loss typical
  • Months 2–3 (0.5–1.0 mg): more noticeable appetite effect, 1–2 lbs/week loss
  • Months 4–6 (1.7–2.4 mg): steady loss, body composition starts to shift
  • Months 6–12: plateau common; dose adjustments or protocol changes

Not everyone responds the same way. About 15–20% of patients are "non-responders" who lose less than 5% at full dose — a signal to consider tirzepatide, which hits an additional receptor (GIP) and typically outperforms semaglutide in head-to-head studies.

Side effects and safety

Common (10%+ of patients): nausea, constipation, diarrhea, fatigue, headache. These are usually mild-to-moderate, dose-dependent, and resolve as your system adjusts over 2–4 weeks on each new dose.

Less common but worth knowing: acid reflux, gallstones (more common with rapid weight loss generally), hypoglycemia if combined with insulin or sulfonylureas, hair thinning (temporary, nutrition-related), muscle loss if protein intake is inadequate.

Rare but serious: pancreatitis (severe abdominal pain → stop and seek care), severe allergic reaction, acute kidney injury (usually from dehydration due to nausea).

The boxed warning on semaglutide concerns thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodent studies. Human data to date does not confirm the same risk, but the MTC/MEN2 contraindication remains absolute as a precaution.

Monitoring: we check HbA1c and lipids at baseline and 90 days. If you're on insulin or a sulfonylurea, those often need dose reduction once semaglutide is active.

Dosing and protocol

Standard titration (adjusts to your tolerance):

  • Weeks 1–4: 0.25 mg once weekly
  • Weeks 5–8: 0.5 mg once weekly
  • Weeks 9–12: 1.0 mg once weekly
  • Weeks 13–16: 1.7 mg once weekly
  • Week 17+: 2.4 mg once weekly (maintenance)

We slow titration if side effects are significant — adding an extra 2–4 weeks at a given dose is common and often the difference between staying on protocol or quitting. Compounded semaglutide (what HRT.org dispenses) comes as a multi-dose vial with calibrated syringes; your provider walks you through the first injection and adjusts dosing based on response and tolerability.

Frequently asked questions

Is compounded semaglutide the same as Ozempic / Wegovy?

The active ingredient is the same molecule. Branded Ozempic and Wegovy are manufactured by Novo Nordisk; compounded semaglutide is prepared by licensed compounding pharmacies and often includes vitamin B12 or similar additives. Clinical outcomes are comparable when dosed equivalently, and compounded options are typically significantly less expensive.

What happens when I stop semaglutide?

Appetite returns toward baseline within a few weeks of stopping. The STEP 4 trial showed roughly two-thirds of lost weight returned within one year of discontinuation without a structured lifestyle plan in place. We work with you on a sustainable taper and maintenance plan if you choose to stop.

Should I take tirzepatide instead?

Tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) hits two receptors — GLP-1 AND GIP — and head-to-head data in the SURPASS and SURMOUNT programs show somewhat greater weight loss on average. Semaglutide is typically less expensive and has longer post-marketing safety data. Your provider helps you decide which is the better fit based on tolerability, cost, and goals.

Can I drink alcohol on semaglutide?

Moderate alcohol is generally tolerated but comes with caveats. Many patients report dramatically reduced desire for alcohol on GLP-1 therapy — this is an interesting ongoing area of research. More acute concerns: alcohol can worsen nausea and the risk of pancreatitis. If you drink, we recommend keeping it modest and avoiding binge patterns.
